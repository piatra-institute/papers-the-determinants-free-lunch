## Abstract

Agents that each hold one row of an invertible matrix over a finite field, and see only their own row and a neighbour's, can preserve the determinant without observing it: subtracting multiples of rows and exchanging rows with a sign change are determinant-one maps of two rows. We ask what supplies this capacity, when it suffices and whether the permanent shares it. On a communication graph, determinant-one moves of adjacent pairs generate the full special linear group on each connected component, so the reachable matrices are those sharing each component's exterior product of rows. Agents can reach a form whose determinant is readable from per-row summaries exactly when the pivot profiles of the components' row spaces are disjoint, and a randomised local protocol then reaches it with probability one. Exhaustive checks over 1,381,440 matrix-graph pairs and 400 seeded trials over $\mathbb{F}_{101}$ agree with these theorems. For the permanent we prove that the only linear maps of two rows preserving it in every matrix of size at least three are scalings and exchanges, against all of $\mathrm{SL}_2$ for the determinant, so linear permanent-preserving agents never create a zero. Moves that depend on the state remove this rigidity at small sizes: every $3\times 3$ matrix over $\mathbb{F}_5$ with nonzero permanent can reach a readable form, while greedy agents with access to every permanent-preserving move complete 94 of 200 random $6\times 6$ matrices over $\mathbb{F}_3$. The results do not bear on whether VP differs from VNP.

## A global value that no agent can see

Give each of $n$ agents one row of an invertible matrix over a finite field. An agent sees its own row and, during a contact, the row of one neighbour in a communication graph. No agent sees the matrix, its determinant or the graph. Can such agents transform the matrix into a form from which the determinant can be read off, without ever changing the determinant on the way?

For the determinant the answer is yes, and the reason is elementary: subtracting a multiple of one row from another, or exchanging two rows while negating one of them, multiplies the determinant by one. Two agents can apply these moves using only their own two rows, so a global quantity is preserved by operations that are each local and blind to it. The same question for the permanent, $\operatorname{perm} A=\sum_{\sigma}\prod_i a_{i\sigma(i)}$, has a different answer, and the difference is the subject of this paper.

The question comes from an analogy. Zhang, Goldstein and Levin implemented classical sorting algorithms as distributed policies executed by each array element, introduced "frozen" elements that fail to move when the policy says they should, and reported that the distributed versions tolerated these faults better than the traditional versions and showed behaviour not explicitly encoded in the algorithm, such as temporarily moving away from sortedness [@zhang2024]. Levin interprets capacities of this kind as a "free lunch": once a system has the right physical interface, mathematical facts supply features that did not have to be built separately, a view he connects to a latent space of patterns [@levin2025, p. 16]. This paper treats that vocabulary as Levin's framing and asks a narrower question that can be settled. For the determinant, what exactly supplies the capacity of local agents to make global progress, when does that capacity fail, and does the permanent share it?

The answer has three parts. The capacity comes from the group of determinant-one row operations, and on any communication graph the moves of adjacent pairs generate the whole of that group on each connected component. Whether the agents can reach a readable form is decided by an exact criterion that combines the graph with the information each component holds. For the permanent, the linear moves of a row pair that preserve it in every matrix are only scalings and exchanges, so they never create a zero. When agents may instead choose any permanent-preserving replacement of their two rows as a function of what they see, the picture changes at small sizes, and the measured advantage of the determinant appears only as $n$ grows.

Related work spans three areas that rarely meet. Distributed computing has studied systems in which local steps maintain a global property: Dijkstra's self-stabilising systems keep the relation "the system is in a legitimate state" invariant under the steps of loosely coupled processes [@dijkstra1974]; gossip algorithms compute averages through random pairwise exchanges [@boyd2006]; algebraic gossip spreads messages through random linear coding over finite fields [@deb2006]; population protocols characterise what pairwise interactions of finite-state agents can compute [@angluin2006]; and generic pairwise elimination has been parallelised with attention to communication cost [@tiskin2007]. Algebraic complexity theory separates the determinant, computable by polynomial-size circuits, from the permanent, which is complete for Valiant's class VNP in characteristic other than two [@valiant1979classes; @burgisser2000]; computing the permanent of a 0-1 matrix is #P-complete [@valiant1979permanent]. Whether VP differs from VNP is open. Geometric complexity theory recasts such lower-bound questions as questions about orbits under reductive group actions [@mulmuley2001]. The classical linear preserver theorems identify the global symmetries involved: the linear maps preserving the determinant of every matrix are $X\mapsto UXV$ or $X\mapsto UX^{\top}V$ with $\det(UV)=1$, a result that goes back to Frobenius and was extended to general fields by Dieudonné [@li2001; @dieudonne1949], while the permanent's linear preservers are products of permutation and diagonal matrices, possibly with a transpose [@marcus1962; @botta1967]. The results below are a localised version of this contrast: which symmetries are available to two rows acting alone, and what a network of such pairs can then achieve.

## Agents, moves and readable forms

Fix a prime field $\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_p$ (the algebraic statements hold over any field of characteristic other than two unless stated otherwise) and an invertible $n\times n$ matrix $A$ with rows $r_1,\dots,r_n$. Agent $i$ holds $r_i$. A communication graph $G$ on $\{1,\dots,n\}$ says which pairs can make contact. A pair move on the edge $\{i,j\}$ replaces $(r_i,r_j)$ by a pair $(u,v)$ computed from $(r_i,r_j)$ alone. It is determinant-preserving (permanent-preserving) if the determinant (permanent) of every $n\times n$ matrix that contains $r_i$ and $r_j$ in rows $i$ and $j$ is unchanged, whatever the other rows are.

The lead of a nonzero row is the index of its first nonzero entry. A matrix is readable when its rows have pairwise distinct leads. Sorting the rows by lead then gives an upper triangular matrix, so $\det A=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\prod_i r_i[\operatorname{lead}(r_i)]$, where $\pi$ is the sorting permutation, and $\operatorname{perm}A=\prod_i r_i[\operatorname{lead}(r_i)]$. An observer needs only the triple (agent, lead, pivot value) from each agent. When leads repeat, these summaries do not determine the determinant: some column $j$ is nobody's lead, and multiplying column $j$ by $t\neq 1$ changes no lead and no pivot value while multiplying the determinant by $t$.

For a subspace $W\subseteq\mathbb{F}^n$, its pivot profile $P(W)$ is the set of leads of its nonzero vectors. Row reduction shows that $P(W)$ is the set of pivot columns of any echelon basis of $W$, so $|P(W)|=\dim W$. For a connected component $C$ of $G$ let $W_C$ be the span of the rows held in $C$ and $P_C=P(W_C)$. Define the obstruction index
$$q(A,G)=n-\Big|\bigcup_{C} P_C\Big|.$$
Because the rows are independent, $\sum_C|P_C|=n$, so $q=0$ exactly when the profiles of the components are pairwise disjoint.

The protocol used in the experiments is a specific determinant-preserving policy. On a contact along $\{i,j\}$ with $i<j$: if the two leads are equal to $c$, agent $j$ sets $r_j\leftarrow r_j-(r_j[c]/r_i[c])\,r_i$; if they differ, the pair performs the signed swap $(r_i,r_j)\leftarrow(r_j,-r_i)$ with probability $\beta$ and otherwise does nothing. Both operations act on the pair by a $2\times 2$ matrix of determinant one. An unsigned swap has determinant $-1$ and would flip the sign of $\det A$.

## What local agents can reach

**Theorem 1 (orbits).** Let $\Gamma_G$ be the group generated by all pair moves $(r_i,r_j)\mapsto(ar_i+br_j,\,cr_i+dr_j)$ with $ad-bc=1$ along edges of $G$. Then $\Gamma_G=\prod_C \mathrm{SL}_{|C|}(\mathbb{F})$, each factor acting on the rows held in one component. Consequently a matrix $B$ is reachable from an invertible $A$ exactly when, for every component $C$, the exterior product of the rows of $B$ held in $C$, taken in index order, equals that of $A$.

*Proof.* Each generator is block diagonal with determinant-one blocks, which gives one inclusion. For the other, write $E_{ij}(t)$ for the move $r_j\leftarrow r_j+t\,r_i$. For distinct $i,k,j$ the commutator of $E_{ik}(s)$ and $E_{kj}(t)$ is $E_{ij}(\pm st)$, so by induction along a path every $E_{ij}(t)$ with $i,j$ in the same component lies in $\Gamma_G$. These elementary transvections generate $\mathrm{SL}_{|C|}(\mathbb{F})$ over any field. For the orbit statement, the rows of $A$ in $C$ form a basis of $W_C$, and two ordered bases of the same subspace differ by a determinant-one change of basis exactly when their exterior products agree; a nonzero decomposable exterior product determines its subspace. $\square$

The determinant itself is recovered from the component invariants, since $\det A$ is, up to the sign of a fixed reordering, the exterior product of the component invariants. Theorem 1 therefore lists everything local determinant-one moves preserve: the Plücker coordinates of each component's rows, and nothing else.

**Theorem 2 (readability).** The orbit $\Gamma_G A$ contains a readable matrix if and only if $q(A,G)=0$. More generally, every matrix in the orbit has at most $n-q$ distinct leads, and some matrix in the orbit has exactly $n-q$.

*Proof.* Every row held in $C$ lies in $W_C$, so its lead lies in $P_C$, and the leads of any matrix in the orbit lie in $\bigcup_C P_C$, a set of $n-q$ columns. Conversely, an echelon basis of $W_C$ has leads exactly $P_C$; rescaling one of its vectors matches the exterior product of $A$'s rows in $C$ without changing any lead, and Theorem 1 places the resulting matrix in the orbit. Its set of leads is $\bigcup_C P_C$. $\square$

The criterion depends on the graph and the data together. A disconnected graph can still permit completion when its components hold complementary information: if one component's rows span vectors supported on columns $\{1,2\}$ onward and the other's have profile $\{3,4\}$, the profiles are disjoint and $q=0$. Two components whose row spaces share a profile column cannot both supply it, whatever the agents do.

**Theorem 3 (the protocol).** Suppose the graph is fixed, each edge is offered for contact at each step with probability at least $p_{\min}>0$ independently of the past, and $0<\beta\le 1$. If $q=0$ the protocol reaches a readable matrix with probability one, and once readable it stays readable. If $q>0$ it never reaches one, and with probability one it reaches the bound $n-q$ of Theorem 2.

*Proof.* Let $\Phi=\sum_i\operatorname{lead}(r_i)$. A swap leaves $\Phi$ unchanged and an elimination strictly increases the recipient's lead (the recipient stays nonzero because the rows stay independent), so $\Phi$ increases at most $n(n-1)$ times. Suppose some component contains two rows with the same lead $\ell$. Choose such a pair at minimum distance in $G$ and a shortest path between them. No interior vertex of the path holds a row with lead $\ell$, so the signed swaps along the path are permitted, carry one of the two rows (possibly negated, which keeps its lead) next to the other in at most $L-1$ contacts, where $L$ is the largest component size minus one, and the next contact eliminates. This sequence has probability at least $\alpha=p_{\min}^{L}\beta^{L-1}>0$ in every block of $L$ offers, whatever happened before, so an increase of $\Phi$ occurs with probability one. After the last increase every component has distinct leads, which by Theorem 2 are exactly $P_C$. Contacts only occur inside components, so no further elimination happens and swaps only permute rows within components. If $q=0$ this state is readable; if $q>0$, Theorem 2 rules out readability. $\square$

The proof gives a finite expected completion time, but the bound $n(n-1)L/\alpha$ grows exponentially with component size and predicts nothing useful about observed times. The protocol assumes atomic, exact pair updates on a fixed graph. It does not cover concurrent stale reads, arithmetic faults or graphs that change during the run, and the agents cannot detect that they have finished: an external observer collects the summaries.

## Exhaustive and randomised checks

The theorems were checked by exhaustive enumeration and by seeded randomised trials, implemented from scratch for this paper. For every invertible $3\times 3$ matrix over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and $\mathbb{F}_3$ and every invertible $4\times 4$ matrix over $\mathbb{F}_2$, on every labelled communication graph, the enumeration built the protocol's transition graph and computed which states can reach a readable state, which reach one almost surely (no reachable trap), and whether the deficit $n-q$ is attained. It also computed the orbits of the full pair-local $\mathrm{SL}_2$ action by union-find and compared them with the classes defined by the component exterior products. Across 1,381,440 matrix-graph pairs and 4,007,520 nontrivial transitions there were no disagreements with Theorems 1 to 3, no change of determinant, no change of any component's pivot profile, and no error in the readout from summaries.

The randomised experiment used exact arithmetic over $\mathbb{F}_{101}$ with sizes $n\in\{4,6,8,12,16\}$ and four conditions, each with 20 matrices per size: a uniformly random invertible matrix on the complete graph; a random invertible matrix on a ring with $n$ random chords from which each edge was deleted with probability 0.3, conditioned on staying connected; a matrix built so that two disconnected components have disjoint profiles, with each component's rows hidden by a random invertible mixing; and a random invertible matrix on the same kind of two-component graph. Contacts were offered uniformly over surviving edges with $\beta=1/2$ and a budget of $50n^3$ offers.

All 300 cases with $q=0$ reached a readable form, and none of the 100 cases with $q>0$ did; their obstruction indices ranged from 1 to 7 (Figure 1a). In all 400 cases the final number of missing leads equalled the predicted $q$. All 13,955 determinant audits along the trajectories, and every final readout, agreed with the initial determinant. The slowest completion took 7,603 contacts at $n=16$ against a budget of 204,800. Without swaps, elimination alone completed all 100 complete-graph cases but only 2 of the 100 damaged connected cases: on a sparse graph, rows with equal leads that are not adjacent never meet. Completion times grew roughly polynomially in $n$ under all three feasible conditions (Figure 1b). The randomised trials test the implementation and illustrate the theorems; the theorems rest on their proofs.

## The local symmetries of the determinant and the permanent

Theorem 1 shows where the determinant's local capacity comes from: a two-row symmetry group, $\mathrm{SL}_2$, whose copies along the edges generate a large global symmetry group. The next theorem identifies the corresponding two-row group for the permanent.

**Theorem 4 (pair-local linear preservers).** Let $\mathbb{F}$ have characteristic other than two, let $n\ge 3$, and let $\Phi$ be a linear map of pairs of rows. Then $\Phi$ preserves the determinant of every $n\times n$ matrix containing the pair if and only if $\Phi(x,y)=(ax+by,\,cx+dy)$ with $g=\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{psmallmatrix}$ satisfying $g^{\top}Jg=J$, $J=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{psmallmatrix}$, that is $\det g=1$. It preserves the permanent of every such matrix if and only if $\Phi$ has the same form with $g^{\top}Kg=K$, $K=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{psmallmatrix}$, that is $g=\begin{psmallmatrix}\alpha&0\\0&\alpha^{-1}\end{psmallmatrix}$ or $g=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&\alpha\\ \alpha^{-1}&0\end{psmallmatrix}$.

*Proof.* Laplace expansion along the two rows writes the determinant (permanent) of the matrix as $\sum_{s<t}\pm\varphi_{st}(x,y)\,M_{st}$, where $\varphi_{st}(x,y)=x_sy_t-x_ty_s$ (respectively $x_sy_t+x_ty_s$) and $M_{st}$ is the complementary $(n-2)\times(n-2)$ minor (permanent) of the other rows. The $M_{st}$ are multilinear polynomials in the other rows' entries with pairwise disjoint sets of monomials, and a multilinear polynomial that vanishes everywhere on $\mathbb{F}$ is zero. Hence $\Phi$ preserves the value for every completion if and only if $\varphi_{st}(\Phi(x,y))=\varphi_{st}(x,y)$ for all $s<t$ and all $x,y$.

Write $\Phi(x,y)=(L,M)$ with coordinates $L_s,M_s$, linear forms in the $2n$ variables. Since $|\mathbb{F}|\ge 3$, the identity $L_sM_t\pm L_tM_s=\varphi_{st}$ holds as polynomials. The quadratic form $\varphi_{st}$ has rank four and involves exactly $x_s,x_t,y_s,y_t$. The symmetric matrix of $L_sM_t\pm L_tM_s$ has its row space inside the span of $L_s,M_t,L_t,M_s$, so these four forms are independent and span $\langle x_s,x_t,y_s,y_t\rangle$. Taking two different partners $t,u$ of $s$, which needs $n\ge 3$, gives $\langle L_s,M_s\rangle=\langle x_s,y_s\rangle$: each new column depends only on the same old column, $(L_s,M_s)^{\top}=g_s(x_s,y_s)^{\top}$. Substituting, $\varphi_{st}(\Phi)=w_s^{\top}g_s^{\top}Hg_tw_t$ with $w_s=(x_s,y_s)^{\top}$ and $H=J$ (respectively $K$), so $g_s^{\top}Hg_t=H$ for all $s\neq t$. With three indices this forces all $g_s$ to be one matrix $g$ with $g^{\top}Hg=H$. For $H=J$ this is $\det g=1$. For $H=K$, writing $g=\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{psmallmatrix}$ gives $2ac=0$, $2bd=0$ and $ad+bc=1$, whose solutions are the two monomial families. The converse is a direct check. $\square$

The determinant's two-row group is the isometry group of an alternating form, which in dimension two is all of $\mathrm{SL}_2$ and contains the transvections that create zeros. The permanent's is the isometry group of a split symmetric form, a one-dimensional torus extended by an exchange, which contains no transvection. In characteristic two the forms coincide, the permanent equals the determinant, and the permanent's local group becomes $\mathrm{SL}_2$ as well.

An exhaustive search confirmed Theorem 4 on small cases without assuming linearity of any particular shape. A quadratic map is preserved by a linear map exactly when its values on a basis and its polar form on pairs of basis vectors are preserved, so a backtracking search over images of basis vectors finds every linear preserver. For $n=3$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$ it found 24 determinant preservers and 4 permanent preservers; over $\mathbb{F}_5$, 120 and 8; for $n=4$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$, again 24 and 4. These are the orders of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$ and of the monomial group of Theorem 4, and every preserver found had the stated form. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ both searches found the 6 elements of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{F}_2)$. For $n=2$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$ the search found 1,152 determinant preservers, because when the pair is the whole matrix column operations are also local; this is why the theorem needs $n\ge 3$.

**Corollary 5.** On a connected graph, linear permanent-preserving pair moves generate exactly the maps $A\mapsto PDA$ with $P$ a permutation matrix and $D$ diagonal with $\det D=1$. Every row keeps its set of nonzero positions up to relabelling of rows, no move creates or destroys a zero, and a readable form is reachable only from a matrix that is already readable up to the order of its rows.

The fraction of matrices that are readable is small: a matrix over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is readable for exactly $n!\,(p-1)^n p^{n(n-1)/2}$ of the $p^{n^2}$ matrices. Determinant-preserving agents on a connected graph reach a readable form from every invertible matrix.

## When agents may choose their move from what they see

The protocol's coefficient $r_j[c]/r_i[c]$ depends on the rows, so the moves agents actually make are state-dependent. For the determinant this changes nothing. If a pair move, linear or not, preserves the determinant for every completion, then at each linearly independent pair $(x,y)$ it satisfies $u\wedge v=x\wedge y$ for its output $(u,v)$, so $\langle u,v\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle$ and $(u,v)$ is obtained from $(x,y)$ by an element of $\mathrm{SL}_2$ chosen by the state. Every independent pair has exactly $|\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)|=p(p^2-1)$ admissible replacements, which the census confirmed for $p=3$ and $p=5$ at $n=3,4,5$.

For the permanent, the admissible replacements of a pair are all pairs with the same $2\times 2$ permanents, a set that contains the monomial orbit of Theorem 4 and can be larger. Call a pair frozen when the two coincide. A frozen pair stays frozen under scalings and exchanges, so a matrix all of whose row pairs are frozen can only reach its Corollary 5 orbit, even with state-dependent moves.

The census shows that freezing depends strongly on size. At $n=3$ no independent pair is frozen, and the exhaustive reachability computation over all $3\times 3$ matrices over $\mathbb{F}_5$ found that every one of the 1,513,600 matrices with nonzero permanent can reach a readable form through permanent-preserving pair moves, on the complete graph and on the path alike, with the matrices of each permanent value forming a single class. Only 48,000 of them are readable to begin with, which is what linear moves can exploit. At $n=3$ the permanent's local rigidity is therefore a property of fixed linear moves and disappears once moves may depend on the state. At $n=5$ over $\mathbb{F}_5$, 6,328,320 of the 9,746,880 independent pairs are frozen, and 458 of 20,000 uniformly random matrices have all ten row pairs frozen. Over these small fields many pairs are special, so most matrices still admit some extra move. A dimension count suggests that frozen pairs become generic for $n\ge 5$ over large fields, because the $n(n-1)/2$ constraints on the $2n$ coordinates of the replacement then outnumber the free parameters left after the one-dimensional torus; this was not proved.

To measure what the extra moves buy, the last experiment gave agents on the complete graph an oracle for every value-preserving replacement of their two rows and let each contact choose, uniformly among the replacements, one that maximises the sum of the two leads. For the determinant this reproduces elimination, and these agents completed all 200 random matrices in every setting tested. For the permanent they completed all 200 at $n=3$ and $n=4$ over $\mathbb{F}_5$, but 118 of 200 at $n=5$ over $\mathbb{F}_5$, 162 of 200 at $n=5$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$, and 94 of 200 at $n=6$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$, each with a budget of 3,000 contacts (Figure 1d). The greedy rule is one policy, so a failure shows that this rule stalls, not that no sequence of permanent-preserving moves exists.

## Faults in the sign rule

A different way to perturb the determinant, closer to injecting faults into a sorting procedure, is to corrupt its sign rule. For a set $E$ of row pairs let
$$D_E(A)=\sum_{\sigma}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}_E(\sigma)}\prod_i a_{i\sigma(i)},$$
where $\operatorname{inv}_E(\sigma)$ counts the pairs $\{i<j\}\in E$ with $\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)$. Then $D_\emptyset=\det$ and $D_E=\operatorname{perm}$ when $E$ contains all pairs. Pólya asked in 1913 when signs can be attached to the entries of a matrix so that its permanent becomes the determinant of the signed matrix, and Szegő showed that for every $n\ge 3$ some $n\times n$ matrix admits no such signs [@mccuaig2004, p. 11]. Vazirani and Yannakakis showed that a 0-1 matrix admits such signs exactly when its bipartite graph has a Pfaffian orientation, a notion due to Kasteleyn [@vazirani1989; @kasteleyn1963]; Little characterised these graphs by excluded $K_{3,3}$ configurations [@little1975]; and Robertson, Seymour and Thomas, and McCuaig, gave structural characterisations that yield polynomial-time recognition [@robertson1999; @mccuaig2004]. Those results restrict the support of the matrix and keep the permanent. The family $D_E$ keeps every entry and changes the sign rule, so it asks a different question.

**Proposition 6.** $D_E$ equals the determinant after a change of signs of matrix entries (and a global sign) if and only if $E$ is a cut of the complete graph, $E=\{\{i,j\}: i\in S,\ j\notin S\}$ for some $S$. It equals the permanent after such a change if and only if the complement of $E$ is a cut.

*Proof.* The coefficient of $\prod_i a_{i\sigma(i)}$ in $D_E$ is $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}_E(\sigma)}$, so $D_E$ is a signed determinant exactly when $(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}_E(\sigma)}=c\prod_i s_{i\sigma(i)}$ for all $\sigma$. For a cut, counting crossing inversions modulo two gives $\operatorname{inv}_E(\sigma)\equiv\sum_{i\in S}(i+\sigma(i))+\text{const}$, which has this form. Conversely, restricting to permutations that move only three rows $i<j<k$ among the columns $\{i,j,k\}$ turns inversions with fixed rows into entry-dependent signs, so $E$ restricted to $\{i,j,k\}$ must itself be admissible for $n=3$; enumeration of the eight graphs on three vertices shows this means $|E\cap\{ij,ik,jk\}|$ is even. A set of edges of the complete graph meeting every triangle evenly is a cut, because triangles span its cycle space over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$. The permanent case is the same argument for the pairs that keep their sign. $\square$

The case $E$ equal to all pairs, for $n\ge 3$, is Szegő's theorem for a matrix of independent entries. Because the condition is linear in $E$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, it can be checked for all $2^{n(n-1)/2}$ sign rules at once. For $n=3,\dots,7$ the admissible sets of the computation were exactly the cuts; at $n=7$, 64 of the 2,097,152 rules give a signed determinant and another 64 a signed permanent.

The remaining rules sit between the two polynomials, and a simple projection shows where hardness enters. If $E$ contains every pair inside a set $T$ of rows, setting each row outside $T$ to its unit vector and the entries of rows in $T$ outside the columns of $T$ to zero gives $D_E(A)=\operatorname{perm}(S\circ A_T)$ for an explicit sign matrix $S$. A circuit for $D_E$ therefore yields a circuit of the same size, plus sign changes, for the permanent of size $|T|$, so a family $D_{E_n}$ whose clique sizes grow polynomially in $n$ is as hard as the permanent up to polynomial factors. All 20 numerical checks of this identity, at $n=6$ and $n=7$, passed. Whether rules without large cliques, and without cut structure, can be computed efficiently was not determined.

## What the free lunch amounts to

Theorems 1 to 4 make one reading of the free lunch exact. The determinant does not have to be known, estimated or transmitted for local agents to protect it: each permitted move is a determinant-one map of two rows, and these maps generate the full determinant-one group on every connected component. That group is large enough that its orbits are the level sets of a short list of invariants, and among its elements are the transvections that eliminate entries. The permanent's two-row linear symmetries are scalings and exchanges, which preserve every zero pattern. In that sense the determinant's local capacity is a consequence of its symmetry group, and the permanent lacks the corresponding local moves.

The lunch has costs. Each contact moves an entire row, $O(n)$ field symbols, and each elimination costs $O(n)$ field operations. Certifying feasibility requires the global profiles $P_C$, which no agent holds. Reading the answer requires collecting all summaries, and the agents have no way to detect completion. Sparse graphs need many contacts, and without the signed swap that routes rows through the graph, elimination stalls. The determinant-preserving moves were supplied by the designer; the agents did not discover them.

The permanent's missing moves are a statement about fixed linear moves. The previous sections show that state-dependent permanent-preserving moves can make full progress at $n=3$ and substantial progress at $n=4$ and $n=5$ over small fields. What separates the two polynomials in these experiments is not whether value-preserving local moves exist but whether they form a group acting uniformly on all states, with a closed-form choice such as $r_j[c]/r_i[c]$, or have to be found by solving quadratic equations pair by pair, with the solutions thinning out as $n$ grows.

None of this bears directly on VP versus VNP, which remains open. An efficient algorithm for the permanent need not preserve the permanent at intermediate steps, so the absence of permanent-preserving eliminations is not a lower bound. The pair-local theorems are localised versions of the classical global preserver theorems, and the collective protocol is a distributed form of Gaussian elimination; their contribution is an exact, testable account of when locally blind agents can expose a global invariant and of which symmetry makes that possible. The experiments establish this for one designed protocol, a family of greedy policies and small fields. They do not measure causal emergence in any formal sense, and they show no learning, planning or behaviour outside what the stated rules imply. Two questions remain open where the computations stop: a useful prediction of completion time from graph bottlenecks, and whether frozen pairs are generic for $n\ge 5$ over large fields, which would turn the permanent's state-dependent rigidity from a measured trend into a theorem.
